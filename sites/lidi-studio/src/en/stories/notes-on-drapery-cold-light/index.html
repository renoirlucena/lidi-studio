Notes on Drapery, in Cold Light

A working note from the studio. On wardrobe, on layered light, on what it means to slow down a portrait.

People sometimes ask why I spend so long on wardrobe before a session. The honest answer is that wardrobe is half of the photograph. The drape of a gown, the way it catches light, the way it falls when a body shifts — these are not afterthoughts. They are the photograph, almost as much as the face is.

Why drapery matters

A portrait painter, working from life, spends as much time arranging the cloth as arranging the figure. There's a reason for that. Cloth is what the eye reads first. It tells the viewer where to look, where to rest, where the figure ends and the air begins. Done well, drapery does most of the composing for you. Done thoughtlessly, it flattens everything into a snapshot.

I keep a small studio collection of dresses, gowns, and lengths of cloth. Some are mine; some I've made; some I've found over the years. None are arbitrary. Every piece has a quality I'm looking for — the way it folds, the weight it carries, the temperature of its color against skin.

Cold light, slow work

Anchorage gives you an unusual gift, photographically: cold light. The blue of a winter morning here doesn't exist most other places. It's directional without being harsh. It rewards patience.

I work slowly with it. I don't bring a strobe to a session unless I have to. I let the room or the window do most of the lighting, then I shape what's there — a card to lift, a sheer to soften. The result is light that looks like it was painted on, because it almost was.

"Cloth is what the eye reads first. Done well, drapery does most of the composing for you."

Choosing wardrobe for a session

Before a session, we have a conversation about wardrobe. I ask what you already own and love. I ask what you've never worn but wish you would. I ask about colors that surprise you, and colors that exhaust you. Then I bring options from the studio collection.

Three rules guide what we choose. First, the wardrobe should serve you, not the other way around. If a piece is more interesting than the person in it, it goes back on the rack. Second, simplicity reads better than complexity in a photograph that's meant to last. A heavy drape in a single tone will outlive a busy print every time. Third, the wardrobe should match the chapter — a maternity sitting wants softness and weight; a motherhood sitting often wants something the mother actually feels like herself in. We work toward both at once.

By the time you arrive at the studio, two or three options are hanging on the dressform, ready. I will have already shaped one of them roughly, just to see how it falls. We adjust together.

What I look for

Through the viewfinder, I'm not looking for a smile or a gesture. I'm looking for a moment when the body forgets it's being photographed — when posture relaxes into composition without performing it. The drapery makes that easier. Cloth gives the body something to be in conversation with, instead of the camera.

That's the whole method, more or less. Wardrobe, light, time. Almost no direction. The portrait builds itself if I've done my preparation.
