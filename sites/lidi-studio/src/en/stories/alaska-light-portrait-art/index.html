Alaska Light: A Portrait Painter's Notes on Anchorage

Why the light here is unlike any other I've photographed in — and how it shapes the work.

People often think of Alaska as a hard place to photograph. Cold, dark half the year, the sun doing strange things on either side of solstice. They're not wrong about any of that, but they're missing the point. Alaska light is unlike anywhere else, and once you've worked with it for a while, going somewhere with normal sun feels almost flat.

Why Anchorage's light is unique

The angle is what does it. We're far enough north that the sun, even at its highest, doesn't quite climb to the harsh overhead position you get in most of the lower 48. The result is a light that's almost always raking — coming in from the side, picking up texture, throwing the long shadows that painters love. Add the cool color temperature of high-latitude air, and you get a register that's quietly painterly without trying to be.

For portrait work, this is a gift. The kind of light that takes hours to set up in a studio in Los Angeles arrives free of charge here, several hours a day, most of the year.

Working with winter light

Winter is what most photographers warn against. I plan around it. The hours between roughly ten in the morning and three in the afternoon, when light exists at all in deep winter, are some of the most extraordinary I've ever worked in. Blue hour stretches into a kind of all-day golden-blue that doesn't quite resolve in either direction. Maternity portraits made in late January often have a quality I can't replicate any other time of year.

The trick is preparation. Sessions in deep winter are short and pre-planned to the minute. We talk about it in advance — wardrobe and timing and where we'll be. The light won't wait.

Summer's long days

The opposite problem in summer. The sun barely sets — what passes for golden hour stretches across most of the evening. This sounds like paradise, and it is, but it has its own discipline. With light available for sixteen hours, the temptation is to use it lazily. I find I work harder to choose the right window: late evening for warmth, early morning for the kind of clarity that suits a newborn portrait.

Studio vs location in Alaska

I work both ways, and I choose between them honestly. The studio gives me total control — drapery, layered light, the particular slowness that a maternity sitting often needs. Location gives me the place itself, which in Alaska is half of any photograph. A motherhood session in Eagle River with the Chugach in the background is a different kind of portrait than one made in front of the dressform.

The decision usually comes from the chapter. Maternity wants studio. Newborn wants studio. Motherhood and family often want location — though not always. We talk it through.

Service area

The studio is in Anchorage. I also work on location across South-Central Alaska — Eagle River, Palmer, Wasilla, Girdwood — and occasionally further afield by arrangement. If you're somewhere on the Mat-Su or the Kenai and the light is right, we can talk about it.

If you're considering a session and want to think about the light first, look through the portfolio — you can see the seasons in the work — or read about how a session unfolds. When you're ready, begin a conversation.
